01Part 1 · 3 min

Water that never leaves

Start here: Earth keeps reusing the same water, and two forces drive the whole loop.

Think about the water in a glass on your table. It is not new. The water in it may once have fallen as snow on a mountain, run down a river, and then spent a few hundred years in the deep ocean before it reached you.

Earth does not make new water, and it loses very little of it to space. So the same water travels again and again between three places: the oceans, the air and the land. This never-ending loop is the water cycle.

Simpler

Earth has had the same water for a very long time. It keeps going round and round: sea, sky, land, and back to the sea.

Key idea

The water cycle is driven by just two things: heat from the Sun, which lifts water up into the air, and gravity, which pulls it back down.

Think of it like thisAdded background

Picture a closed glass jar with a little water and a plant inside, standing in a sunny window. Drops appear on the inside of the lid, run down the sides and wet the soil again. Nothing comes in and nothing goes out, yet the water is always on the move. Earth works the same way, only much bigger.

Almost all of Earth's water is salty: about 97 out of every 100 litres is sea water. Most of the fresh water is frozen in ice caps and glaciers. Only a small share sits in lakes, rivers, soil and the air, and yet that small share is the water that plants, animals and people use every day.

02Part 2 · 2 min

Evaporation: from liquid to vapour

Step 1 of the loop: how water leaves the sea and the land without boiling.

When sunlight warms the surface of the ocean, some water molecules gain enough energy to escape into the air. They become water vapour: water as an invisible gas. The water did not have to boil. At any temperature, the fastest molecules at the surface can break free.

Simpler

The Sun warms the sea. A few tiny bits of water get enough energy to fly off into the air. You cannot see them: they are now a gas.

Some days dry things much faster than others. Warm, dry and windy days speed evaporation up. Warm water gives molecules more energy, dry air has more room for vapour, and wind carries the vapour away so more can follow.

Plants add vapour too. They pull water from the soil through their roots and release it through tiny pores in their leaves. This is called transpiration. A large tree can move a lot of water from the ground into the air on a hot day.

Worked example: Why is rain fresh when most of it started in the salty sea?From your material

Most rain began as sea water, and sea water is salty. So why is rain not salty?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Sea water is water with salt dissolved in it.
  2. When the Sun warms the surface, only the water molecules escape as vapour.
  3. Salt cannot turn into a gas at these temperatures, so it stays behind in the sea.
  4. The vapour later becomes cloud droplets and rain, and it is made of water only.
  5. Answer: Evaporation leaves the salt behind, so the water that rises, and later falls as rain, is fresh.

Common mistakeAdded background

The white cloud above a boiling kettle is not water vapour. Vapour is invisible. What you see is tiny drops of liquid that formed when the vapour met cooler air, which is condensation, the next step.

In short

  • Evaporation turns liquid water into invisible vapour.
  • Warm, dry, windy weather makes it faster.
  • Plants release vapour through their leaves: transpiration.
  • Salt stays behind, so rain is fresh.

03Part 3 · 2 min

Condensation: building clouds

Step 2: why vapour turns back into droplets high in the sky.

Warm air is lighter, so it rises. As it rises, it cools. And cool air cannot hold as much vapour as warm air. The extra vapour has to go somewhere, so it turns back into tiny drops of liquid water.

How a cloud forms, step by step

  1. The Sun warms the ground and the air just above it.
  2. That warm, moist air rises because it is lighter than the air around it.
  3. Higher up, the air cools.
  4. Cool air can hold less vapour, so some vapour becomes liquid.
  5. Each droplet forms around a tiny speck of dust, salt or smoke.
  6. Billions of droplets together scatter sunlight: we see a white cloud.

You can see condensation at home. Take a cold glass out on a summer day and its outside gets wet. The water on the outside came from the air, not from inside the glass. The cold glass cooled the air touching it, and that air had to give up some of its vapour.

Go deeper: The dew pointAdded background

Meteorologists talk about the dew point: the temperature at which a given air would start to form droplets. On a humid day the dew point is close to the air temperature, so even a slightly cooler surface, a glass, a car window, or grass at night, gets wet. This is where morning dew comes from.

Flip cards: tap to check yourself

In short

  • Rising air cools, and cool air holds less vapour.
  • The extra vapour becomes droplets around specks of dust, salt or smoke.
  • A cloud is billions of these droplets.

04Part 4 · 2 min

Precipitation: coming back down

Step 3: how tiny cloud droplets become rain, snow, sleet or hail.

Cloud droplets are so small and light that rising air keeps them up. Inside the cloud they bump into each other and join. When a drop becomes too heavy to stay up, it falls. Water falling from a cloud is called precipitation.

Added background

Worked example: How many cloud droplets fill a bottle?Example to practise

A one-litre bottle holds about 20,000 raindrops (an easy number to practise with). If one raindrop holds about a million cloud droplets, how many cloud droplets are in the bottle?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. One raindrop ≈ 1,000,000 cloud droplets.
  2. The bottle holds about 20,000 raindrops.
  3. Multiply: 20,000 × 1,000,000 = 20,000,000,000.
  4. That is about 20 billion cloud droplets.
  5. Answer: About 20 billion cloud droplets, all joined up, fill one litre bottle.

The source gives the key number: a single raindrop holds about a million cloud droplets. That is why clouds can hang in the sky for hours without raining: it takes a lot of joining before drops are heavy enough to fall.

In short

  • Droplets bump, join and fall when they are too heavy to stay up.
  • Precipitation can be rain, snow, sleet or hail.
  • One raindrop holds about a million cloud droplets.

05Part 5 · 2 min

Collection: run-off and groundwater

Step 4: where the water goes once it lands, and how long it can stay.

Rain that lands on hills and streets flows downhill. This is run-off: water moving over the surface into streams, rivers and finally the sea. Run-off is fast; a heavy storm can fill a river within hours.

Some water does not run off. It soaks into the soil and moves slowly through rock as groundwater. It can stay there for days or for thousands of years. Wells and springs draw on this hidden store.

Think of it like this

Snow on mountains acts like a bank account for water: it saves water in winter, when it falls as snow, and pays it out in spring, when it melts and feeds the rivers.

How long water can stay in one place

PlaceHow long
Deep oceana few hundred years
Groundwaterdays to thousands of years
Mountain snowa winter, until spring
A river after a stormhours to days

Key ideaAdded background

Landing water takes one of two roads: fast over the surface as run-off, or slow through the ground as groundwater. Which road it takes decides whether a storm becomes a flood or refills a well.

In short

  • Run-off flows over the land to rivers and the sea.
  • Groundwater soaks in and moves slowly, for days or thousands of years.
  • Mountain snow stores water in winter and releases it in spring.

06Part 6 · 2 min

Why it matters, at home and at work

The same loop explains floods, droughts and why saving water here connects to rain somewhere else.

The water cycle does more than move water. It moves heat around the planet, shapes the weather, and refills the fresh water we drink.

People change the cycle. When forests are cut, when cities cover soil with concrete, or when the air warms, rain may come in shorter, heavier bursts, and less water may soak into the ground.

Worked example: A car park that keeps floodingExample to practise

A facilities manager notices that the company car park floods after every heavy storm, but the lawn next to it never does. Using the water cycle, what is going on, and what could help?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. The lawn is soil: rain soaks in and becomes groundwater, slowly.
  2. The car park is sealed asphalt: almost all rain becomes run-off, at once.
  3. All that run-off reaches the drains in minutes, more than they can carry, so water pools.
  4. Letting some water soak in (a strip of planted ground, permeable paving, or a rain garden) turns fast run-off back into slow groundwater.
  5. Answer: Sealed surfaces turn rain into fast run-off. Giving the water a place to soak in reduces the flooding.

Common mistakeAdded background

Saving water in your city does not make it rain more there tomorrow. The link is slower and wider: water you leave in rivers and the ground keeps the local part of the cycle healthy, and that water evaporates and falls again somewhere downstream or downwind.

Key idea

Understanding the cycle helps explain floods, droughts, and why saving water in one place connects to rain somewhere else.

In short

  • The cycle moves heat, shapes weather and refills fresh water.
  • Cutting forests, sealing soil and warming air change it: heavier bursts, less soaking in.
  • Floods and droughts are the cycle out of balance.
