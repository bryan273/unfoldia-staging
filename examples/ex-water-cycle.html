The Same Water Keeps Moving

Earth keeps reusing the same water, so understanding its endless journey is the starting point for understanding the water cycle.

Key idea

The water cycle is the endless movement of water between oceans, air and land. Earth makes no new water and loses little to space.

Two things drive this movement: heat from the Sun and gravity, the pull that brings water downward.

Water in your glass today may once have been mountain snow, flowed along a river or spent a few hundred years in the deep ocean.

About 97 out of every 100 litres of Earth's water are salty sea water. Most fresh water is frozen in ice caps and glaciers. Only a small share is in lakes, rivers, soil and air, yet plants, animals and people use that share daily.

Figure note: Figure 1 (page 1)

Follow the loop: ocean evaporation, water becoming gas; condensation, cloud droplets forming; precipitation, water falling over land; and run-off, water flowing back to sea. Some enters the ground as groundwater.

In short

  • The same water moves repeatedly between oceans, air and land.
  • Sun heat and gravity drive the cycle.
  • Most water is salty, and most fresh water is frozen.

Water Enters the Air and Forms Clouds

Evaporation and condensation explain how liquid water enters the air and becomes visible clouds.

Evaporation is liquid water turning into water vapour, an invisible gas. Sunlight warms the ocean surface until some water molecules, the tiny particles that make up water, gain enough energy to escape into the air.

Warm, dry and windy days speed up evaporation. Salt stays behind, explaining why rain is fresh even though most of it began in the sea.

Transpiration is plants releasing water vapour. Roots take up soil water, and tiny pores, or openings, in leaves release the vapour.

Condensation is vapour turning back into tiny liquid droplets. Warm air rises and cools. Cool air cannot hold as much vapour as warm air.

Droplets form around specks of dust, salt or smoke. Billions together make a visible cloud.

Common mistake

Water outside a cold glass comes from vapour in the surrounding air condensing, not from inside the glass.

In short

  • Evaporation and transpiration put water vapour into the air.
  • Condensation turns vapour into droplets that form clouds.

Part 3 · 1 min

Water Falls and Returns to Storage

Water falling from clouds returns to the sea or enters stores that hold it for different lengths of time.

Precipitation means water falling from clouds. Inside clouds, tiny droplets collide and join. When they become too heavy to stay up, they fall as rain.

Key idea

If the air is cold enough, water falls as snow, sleet or hail instead of rain. A single raindrop holds about a million cloud droplets.

Run-off is water flowing over land. Rain landing on hills and streets moves downhill into streams, then rivers, and finally the sea.

Some water soaks into soil and moves slowly through rock as groundwater, water stored underground. It may stay there for days or thousands of years. Wells and springs draw on this hidden store.

Think of it like this

Mountain snow acts like a bank for water: it stores water in winter and releases it in spring.

In short

  • Water falls from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
  • It then flows over land or enters underground stores.
  • Mountain snow stores water until spring.

Why the Cycle Matters

The water cycle connects drinking water, weather and changes people make to land and air.

The water cycle does more than move water: it moves heat around the planet, shapes weather and refills the fresh water people drink. Only a small share of Earth's water is in lakes, rivers, soil and air, yet people, plants and animals use that share every day.

Changes to the cycle

What changesWhat may happen
Forests are cut, cities cover soil with concrete, or the air warms.Rain may arrive in shorter, heavier bursts, and less water may soak into the ground.

Common mistake

Notice the word “may”: these are possible changes. The source does not say that every place will receive shorter, heavier rain or have less water soaking into its ground.

A flood is water covering land that is normally dry. A drought is a long period with unusually little rain.

Key idea

Understanding the cycle helps explain floods and droughts. It also helps us see that saving water in one place connects to rain somewhere else: local water use belongs to a wider cycle.

In short

  • The cycle moves heat, shapes weather and refills drinking water supplies.
  • Changes to forests, city surfaces and air can alter rainfall and how much water enters the ground.
  • Water use in one place connects to the cycle elsewhere.

Glossary

Water cycle
The endless movement of the same water between oceans, air and land, driven by the Sun's heat and gravity.
Gravity
The pull that helps drive the water cycle.
Evaporation
Liquid water turning into water vapour.
Water vapour
Water in the form of an invisible gas.
Transpiration
The release of water vapour by plants through tiny pores in their leaves.
Condensation
Water vapour turning back into tiny droplets of liquid water.
Precipitation
Water falling from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
Run-off
Water flowing over land, downhill into streams, rivers and eventually the sea.
Groundwater
Water stored underground that moves slowly through rock and supplies wells and springs.
